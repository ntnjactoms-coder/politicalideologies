Course overview

This course introduces students to core ideologies that govern politics and acquaints them with contemporary ideologies. It examines the role of ideologies in understanding the state and society in the context of politics. It covers classical ideologies—Liberalism, Conservatism and Socialism—and newer political ideologies—Multiculturalism, Cosmopolitanism and Feminism. A distinctive aspect is its application to the Indian context, with a separate unit addressing Gandhism and Ambedkarism in India.

Course objectives

  • Understand the fundamental concept of political ideology.
  • Critically reflect on political ideology and its functioning in society.
  • Develop an ideological framework for understanding political phenomena.
  • Understand dominant ideologies in the Indian context.

Pedagogy

Apart from lectures and discussions, students will also engage in review of articles and documentary films.

Continuous internal assessment

CIA I totals 20 marks and has two components. CIA III is a longitudinal reflective assessment. The schedule below reproduces the course plan; confirm any dates with the instructor.

CIA I · Component 1

In-class close reading and annotation · 10 marks

Students engage in an in-class close-reading activity based on an instructor-selected text aligned with the syllabus. Each student receives a copy with wider margins for annotation. Students should read carefully and critically, identify key ideas, and record observations in the margins. Different students may receive different texts. This is an individual, in-class assessment. Plagiarism policy: not applicable (in-class assessment).

CIA I · Component 2

Debate with an AI chatbot · 10 marks

Students may choose to debate as either a supporter of liberalism or a critic of liberalism. The AI chatbot takes the opposing position. Students must make the first move by presenting their own opening argument and then continue through sustained exchanges. The complete debate transcript must be submitted. Suggested length: at least 10 substantial exchanges.

Submission deadline: November 27

Suggested initial prompts

Student supporting liberalism

I am a student in a Political Ideologies course. I will participate in a debate as a supporter of liberalism. You should take the role of a thoughtful critic of liberalism. Please engage with me in a serious debate. Challenge my claims, ask questions, and present strong criticisms of liberalism. Do not deliberately make weak arguments simply to let me win. Use very simple, accessible, beginner-friendly language throughout the debate. Write as if you are speaking to first- or second-year undergraduate students taking an introductory course in political theory. Avoid technical philosophical jargon unless it is essential. When you use specialized concepts, explain them briefly in plain language. Do not write like a textbook, journal article, or encyclopaedia. Avoid long paragraphs, complex sentence structures, and unnecessarily sophisticated vocabulary. Keep most responses between 100 and 150 words. Focus on one or two key arguments at a time rather than presenting many ideas at once. Wait for my response before introducing new arguments. I will begin the debate by presenting my first argument.

Student critiquing liberalism

I am a student in a Political Ideologies course. I will participate in a debate as a critic of liberalism. You should take the role of a thoughtful supporter of liberalism. Please engage with me in a serious debate. Challenge my claims, ask questions, and present the strongest arguments in favour of liberalism. Do not deliberately make weak arguments simply to let me win. Keep the discussion accessible and beginner-friendly. Avoid excessive academic jargon and explain unfamiliar concepts when necessary. You may refer to well-known historical events, contemporary developments, public policies, thinkers, or real-world examples to support your arguments. Respond in reasonably short paragraphs. Wait for my response before introducing new arguments. I will begin the debate by presenting my first argument.

Sample opening arguments

These are sample openings provided in the source course plan.

Supporter of liberalism

I support liberalism because I believe people should be free to make important decisions about their own lives. Liberal societies usually allow people to express their views, practice their religion, choose their careers, and participate in politics without excessive interference from the government. Although no society is perfect, I think liberalism does a better job than most alternatives in protecting individual freedom and diversity.

Critic of liberalism

I am critical of liberalism because its emphasis on individual freedom often hides deep inequalities in wealth, power, and social status. While liberalism promises equal rights, many people lack the social or economic resources necessary to exercise those rights meaningfully. As a result, liberal societies may formally guarantee freedom while allowing significant forms of domination and exclusion to persist.

Students will submit a complete debate transcript containing at least 10 substantive exchanges. The student’s responses in the debate with AI need to be original and AI-resistant. The student will be rewarded for openness to learn through the process rather than for the possession of error-free, invulnerable knowledge.

CIA III · Longitudinal reflective assessment · 20 marks

  • Essay 1 (beginning of the semester, 5 marks, 500–800 words): Write a short essay on how you prefer societies to undergo change (for example, gradual and organic changes over a long duration of time, or gradual but active changes pursued through legal, systematic and non-violent political actions, or radical and sweeping changes), and what the role of the state should be in bringing about those changes (for example, the state should not intervene and should leave societies to evolve on their own; the state should actively intervene through constitutionally prescribed measures; the state should use its coercive machinery to bring in social changes). Reflect on how your social location and subjectivity (class or caste location, gender, community, political affiliation, age/life stage) may have shaped this perspective. Rubric: Originality.
  • Essay 2 (towards the end of the semester, 5 marks, 500–800 words): Write a short essay on how you prefer societies to undergo change and what the role of the state should be in bringing about those changes (for example, the state should not intervene and should leave societies to evolve on their own; the state should actively intervene through constitutionally prescribed measures; the state should use its coercive machinery to bring in social changes). Rubric: Originality.
  • Reflective Essay (towards the end of the semester, 10 marks, 500–800 words): Compare your responses in Essay 1 and Essay 2. How do you assess your own intellectual development during the course? Discuss how engagement with political ideologies helped you reaffirm, revise, complicate, or abandon some of your earlier assumptions. Reflect not only on changes in your conclusions but also on changes in how you think about political questions. Rubrics: Originality (5 marks), Critical Thinking (5 marks).

Important: These assignments are intended to reward genuine thinking rather than polished prose. Students will not be penalized for imperfect writing, uncertainty, changing their minds, or making mistakes. However, submissions that are wholly or substantially generated by AI will not be accepted. Such submissions will be returned and must be rewritten independently. An honest but imperfect response is valued more highly than a polished response that does not reflect the student’s own thinking.
